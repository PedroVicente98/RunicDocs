Latency Feel Lab

Pick a ping, then try each lab to feel what it does to action combat. Labs 1–3 are locked in; labs 4–9 are still open. Times follow the server's 30 Hz combat tick, so 1 tick is about 33 ms. Your screen and browser add roughly 20–40 ms on top, as they would in the real game.

5 · Dodge a projectileOpen

An archer shoots an arrow at 30 m/s from 18 m away. Step out of its line before it reaches you. "Real position" draws the arrow where the server has it; otherwise it is drawn like any remote entity, late by the one-way delay plus interpolation. With defender grace, the server checks your position as of the moment you saw the arrow arrive, up to 150 ms.

6 · Smooth remote movementOpen

Snapshots of another player arrive 30 times a second, but the network spreads them unevenly. The client draws remote entities slightly in the past, so it always has two snapshots to blend. Too little delay stutters (the runner freezes and turns red); too much shows the world later than needed. Adaptive delay measures the jitter and keeps only what it needs.

7 · Packet loss on your inputsOpen

Your client sends one input packet per tick, 30 times a second; each square is one packet, newest on the right. Lost packets turn red. With redundancy, each packet repeats the last 3 ticks, so a lost input is usually recovered from the next packet (amber). Without it, a lost dodge is noticed and resent, a round trip later. A tick mark under a square is a change of direction.

8 · Attack warnings in a busy fightOpen

The server sends you attack warnings (red), positions (grey) and chat or inventory updates (amber). With one ordered stream, a lost packet holds every reliable message behind it until it is resent, and warnings wait behind everything else when a fight exceeds your bandwidth. With lanes, warnings travel on their own top-priority lane.
